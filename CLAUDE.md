# Woodshop Simulator

A woodworking sim game: start in a garage with a handsaw, earn money, buy tools, move to bigger shops. Cuts should be accurate enough to plan a real project.

## Pinned versions

Don't upgrade any of these without asking first.

- Rust 1.98.1, edition 2024
- Bevy 0.20.x (`bevy = "0.20.0"` in the workspace `Cargo.toml`)
- proptest 1.12.x

`Cargo.lock` is the source of truth for exact versions. Keep it committed.

## Layout

- `crates/woodsim`: the woodworking simulation. Boards and the exact shape of their faces (`face.rs`), and of their wood where sides lean (`solid.rs`, with 256-bit sums in `wide.rs`), `Length` and `Angle`, kerfs, how a board lies on a saw (`Orientation`), saws, cuts, putting boards together (`assembly.rs`), cutting whatever's on a saw, a board or a glue-up, through one sweep of its blade (`glue_up.rs`), where each board came from (`history.rs`), and wood written out as text and read back exactly (`recipe.rs`). **No Bevy dependency, ever.** Every rule about wood lives here and is tested here.
- `crates/game`: the Bevy app. It draws boards and turns input into `woodsim` calls. It never changes board dimensions itself.
  - Each station is a pair of modules: its state and where to draw the board (plain Rust, no systems, unit tested), and its Bevy side (spawning, input, drawing).
    - Table saw: `feed.rs` (`TableSawStation`: how the board lies, which guide is in use, where the board is along the blade, and the pass under way) and `table_saw.rs`. G switches between the rip fence and the miter gauge; the fence slides out of the way while the gauge is in use. `[` and `]` swing the gauge 1° (with Shift, to its next stop) while the board is clear of the blade; its head, aux fence, and stop turn with it, and so does the board pushed against it (`TableSawStation::gauge_pose`). With the gauge in use, the camera rises to look down over its head and the board, which would hide the blade from the rip fence's lower view (`station_pose`). `,` tilts the blade 1° further and `.` back toward square (with Shift, to its next stop), on a "Blade tilt" card whose left step tilts it further, the way it leans; the blade and riving knife lean about where the blade meets the table (`draw_blade`).
    - Miter saw: `chop.rs` (`MiterSawStation`: the stop, the miter angle, the depth stop, and how far the blade is down) and `miter_saw.rs`. `[` and `]` swing the turntable 1° (with Shift, to its next stop), and the arm and blade turn with it.
    - At both saws, Q/E turn a waiting board a quarter turn, F flips it over, and V stands it up a step at a time: on an edge, then on its end, then back down flat as it first lay (`work::stood_up`); it only stands up wood lying a square way. R chooses what it rests on, so anything, a beveled board or a glue-up at an angle, can lie any way (`rest.rs`, with each station's `work::Rest`): the surface under the pointer lights up yellow, a click rests the wood on it, flat on the table (tipped over onto it the shortest way, then turned the least it can be to bring a surface to the fence), and lights it blue, and a second click picks the surface to go against the fence. While choosing, clicks pick surfaces rather than picking the wood up, and the status says what to click next, or why a surface won't do (`Unsteady`). Both saws take glue-ups too (a glue-up stays flat: V does nothing), through `work.rs`.
  - `work.rs`: what's on a saw, a board or a glue-up alike (`Workpiece`): how far it reaches as it lies (`reach`: the box its wood fills, turned as it lies, which woodsim measures settings from), what a pass does to it (`feed`, `chop`, giving `Passed` and `Parted`), where its boards are drawn (`placed`, `Parted::placed`, `place_glue_up`), and what's handed over when it's carried away (`hand_off`: a glue-up turned as it lay on the saw, so it's set down the same way up; a glue-up the saw left a single board of is that board). A glue-up lies on a saw the way a board would: its `x`, `y`, `z` take the place of a board's length, width, and thickness, and the station's `Orientation` lays them on the saw through `Orientation::rotation`.
    - Workbench: `join.rs` (`BenchStation`: the `Group`s on the bench, each a loose part or parts put together, lying square on the top and kept clear of each other; the surface picked; the joint being lined up; and which surface a click lands on) and `bench.rs`. Parts are set down loose. The player picks every surface themselves, never the game: click a surface of the part that moves, then the surface it goes against. The first part's whole group moves there, shown in orange where it would go: turned whichever of the four ways around the joint leaves it lying square on the top, if any does, and most nearly as it lay (whichever side was up stays up), and slid to the end of the surface the click was nearest, or its middle. Q/E turn it around the joint, arrows slide it between flush and centered (Shift: 1/16"), Enter fastens it (the status says at how many joints, and the reading counts the joints on the bench), Backspace lets go, and U takes the last joint back apart, its parts going back where they lay. Before lining up, Q/E/F turn the part under the pointer (or the picked one) where it lies: the camera only sees surfaces facing it, and the two halves of a miter cut, set down alike, have their miters facing opposite ways. Space picks up a loose part, or the whole glue-up a part is in; a glue-up set back down lies as it was carried, but its joints have set, so U no longer takes them apart. A beveled side is a surface like any other, and so is a face a cut left sloping (`surface_at` finds every surface, the faces too, as a plane: of those facing about the way the click does, the one it lies on, so a slope as gentle as a scarf's isn't mistaken for the top face), and it's a "beveled edge", "beveled end", "beveled miter", or "tapered face": two 45° bevels glue into a square corner, and two scarfs glue end to end. `tests` click every triangle the mesh draws on boards cut every way and check each picks the surface it's on. The surface under the pointer lights up yellow and the picked one blue; their sheets cull their back faces and stop short of the surface's edges, so a picked surface facing away never pokes out past the board. Dragging at the bench turns the view around it, and scrolling zooms (`BenchView`). P draws up plans for the glue-up under the pointer (or the picked part's, or the last put together), as it lies on the bench, and opens them; the status says where they were saved.
  - `plan.rs`: plans to print, drawn up from an assembly (`Plan::of`, `Plan::page`): a web page with a drawing of the whole thing, what lumber to buy, a cut list, cutting diagrams, a drawing of each part to scale from above with its length, width, miter angles, bevels (filled, or dashed where they face down, each labeled "45° bevel"), and slots (where each opens onto the face toward you, dashed where it opens onto the other, and filled where they overlap and it goes through, from `Board::opening`; a slot that opens onto neither, like a resaw slot in an edge, isn't drawn but still keeps the part from matching one without it), and the steps to build it: every cut and every joint in one list, in the order they were made (`Plan::build_steps`, by their numbers), each joint drawn with what went on picked out. It's saved as the next free `plans/plan-N.html` (gitignored) and opened in the browser. Parts that come out the same to a 64th, however they're turned over, are one line of the cut list (`Sketch`), lettered in the order they're in the assembly (A1, A2, ... when there are several). A step is each joint that brought two parts or glue-ups together; joints that only fastened parts already together, like a frame's last corner, go with the step before. The drawings look from in front, to the right, and above, cull back faces, and draw boards in the order a plane through one of their faces separates them. To look at a page yourself, render it with headless Chrome (`"/Applications/Google Chrome.app/Contents/MacOS/Google Chrome" --headless=new --screenshot=out.png --window-size=820,2400 file://...`) and read the image: the browser pane can't screenshot a local file.
  - `cutting.rs`: the cutting half of plans, from where each part came from (`Cutting::of` follows each member's `Origin` back to its stock): the lumber to buy (`buy_list`, by the rack's names, "1x4"), each stock board drawn from above with its parts where they came out of it (`diagrams`, from `at_on_stock`), and each cut in words with the saw set as it was and how the wood lay on it (`describe`, `lying`: on edge, on end, or ", the board resting on its beveled edge, its bottom face to the fence", from `Assembly::rests_on`), a table saw pass named for the board's axis across the table, which the blade is square to (`verb`: through its thickness it resaws, its length it crosscuts, its width it rips): the parts it cuts off by name ("Cut A1 off the rest of the 3/4" strip from step 2"), a cut that only takes the end off as a trim, and what goes on to a later step by a short name (`name`: the lumber, "the 3/4" strip" a rip left, "the 4" piece", or "the rest of" whatever was cut, for more than half of it). Only cuts some part came from are listed, so wood that went to scrap leaves no steps. A pass the saw worked out afresh as the board moved, or the blade went deeper, is one step: passes from the same board are merged, sorted by when the pass began. Cuts that nibbled a board down, each the saw set the same way but no longer, keeping only the measured piece to cut again, are the last of them (`merge_nibbles`). Work done again is listed once (`Listing`): passes one after another along a board, each cut's offcut cut next, are one step ("Rip four 2-3/8" strips off a new 1x12"); a run of two or three cuts repeated along a board is listed once and then "Repeat steps 2 and 3 three more times"; and the same cuts made to several pieces are one step with the cuts as a short list ("For each 2-3/8" strip from step 1, to make A1, A2, A3, and A4:"), with how the piece was turned between cuts on the same saw ("turn it end for end"). Cuts are alike when the saw is set the same way and the same side is kept. `tests` in `plan.rs` make a frame the way you would (rip a 1x4, then trim and cut each piece) and check the page buys one board and lists the cuts, then the joints.
  - `copy.rs`: Copy (Y, or the button beside the inventory's) writes what's in hand, or what's at the station you're at, as a `woodsim::Recipe` for Claude: on a saw, the wood as it was before the pass under way, with the saw set as it was for it, how the wood lies, and how far the pass went, so the pass can be made again; at the bench, the picked part's glue-up. Comments on top say what it is and what the status said. It goes on the clipboard (`pbcopy`, `clip`, or `wl-copy`/`xclip`) and is saved as the next free `pieces/piece-N.txt` (gitignored), and a `Notice` says where for a few seconds. When the user pastes one, read it in a test with `Recipe::read` and run the `saw` line's pass on it to reproduce what they saw.
  - `shop.rs`: the shop around the stations: layout helpers (`Block`, `inches`, `rounded_block`), shared textures and materials (`ShopAssets`), the floor, and the camera. A station reports what's on it as `SawBoards`: for each part (whole, piece, offcut), the `Placed` boards in it, one for a board and one per board of a glue-up, each drawn by an entity with that `Part` and its `Slot`; `place_parts` hands back the boards no entity draws yet, for the station to spawn. A `Placed` is the `woodsim::Board`, where its corner sits and how it's turned, in the saw's own frame (across toward the fence, up, along as x, y, z). `Placed::lying` lays a board down as its `Orientation` says, `Placed::piece` puts a cut piece where it was on its board, and `place_parts` turns the saw's frame into the station's and draws them. `describe_board` puts a board, its mitered ends, and its slots into words, with a mitered board's length to its long points; it reads kerfs cut side by side the same way as one wider slot (a dado nibbled out a stroke at a time) and counts slots that measure the same. A part's `Shape` remembers the board its mesh was built from, so meshes are only rebuilt when a board changes, not every frame the blade moves.
    - `ShopView` is what the camera shows: the `Overview` of the whole shop (the starting view; pan, zoom, rotate) or one station up close: the table saw, the miter saw, or the workbench, which Tab (or the tabs along the top of the screen) goes around. A station's controls only run while it's up close; mouse controls work from either.
    - Each station's coordinates start at its own `ORIGIN`, and its `BOUNDS` outline it in the overview. Pass its body's entities to `station_part` so clicking them zooms in.
  - `scenery.rs`: everything that's just for looks: walls that cut away on the camera's side, the sun and ambient light, the yard, the lumber rack, pegboard, and props.
  - `controls.rs`: every control is an `Action` (turn the board, step the fence, fasten, go to a station, ...), and `bound` says which key does it at each view. `read_keys` fills `Actions` with what started this frame and what's held (pushing a board, lowering the miter saw's blade, panning), and buttons add to it; stations act on `Actions`, never on keys, so a button and its key do exactly the same thing. A new control is an `Action`, a key in `bound` (and `KEYS`, for the badge and help), and a button in the HUD.
  - `hud.rs`: everything drawn over the shop, like a game's HUD rather than text instructions. Along the top, a button home and a tab for each station, the frame rate, and buttons for the inventory (I) and the controls sheet (H). At a station, its settings down the left as cards (`cards`), each with step buttons: single chevrons a small step, double a big one (what Shift does to the key). Along the bottom, the dock of round icon buttons for what can be done there, in groups (`dock`), under a line on what's going on (the station's `status`). What's in hand shows at the top, with a button to put it away. Down the right, the inventory (`build_inventory`, rebuilt when it or the view changes): a line per kind of piece on each shelf, counted together, with buttons to use it here, move it to the other shelf, or throw it away, then new lumber; its list scrolls under the mouse wheel (`scroll_inventory`), and scrolling over the HUD doesn't zoom the view. Each button (`spawn_button`) shows its key in a badge and what it does in a tooltip (`Shop::describe`; one tooltip for every button, `show_tip`, placed beside whichever is under the pointer so a button in the scrolling list isn't clipped), dims and stops working when it would do nothing (`Shop::can`), lights up for a choice that's made (`Shop::lit`: the station, the fastener, the guide in use), and can hide (`Shop::shown`: the slide arrows only show while lining up a joint). A held action keeps going while its button is pressed. `OnHud` says whether a press started on the HUD, so a drag from a button doesn't turn the bench view. Statuses talk about what to do, not which key does it: the buttons and the controls sheet say that.
  - `icons.rs`: every button's icon, painted in code at startup from strokes and filled shapes in a unit square (`Icon::shapes`), white so the button tints it, mipmapped. A test checks each covers a sensible part of its square. `picture` paints a piece of wood the same way, on demand: a board or glue-up from above, pine edged in brown, fitted to the square, with a board too thin to see drawn thick enough to.
  - `flights.rs`: wood shown moving, so it's clear where it went. The inventory sends a `Moved` message for every piece that goes in or out, saying where from and to (`Spot`: a station, its line in the inventory, the button just clicked, the hand, or gone), and a picture of it flies there along an arc, shrinking into the inventory and growing out of it. Where it lands, its line flashes, or the inventory's button bounces if the line isn't in view. What's in hand hangs just under the pointer as a picture, popping in when it's picked up, and the in-hand card shows it too. Positions are worked out each frame, in logical pixels: a station's from where its wood is (`Screen::wood_at`), projected through the camera. Headless runs have no pointer, so to see what's in hand in a screenshot, temporarily make `Screen::pointer` return a fixed point.
  - `textures.rs`: every texture is generated in code at startup (wood, floor tiles, pegboard, grass). There are no image files.
  - `fonts/`: the one file the game ships, Nunito (variable weight, SIL Open Font License in `OFL.txt`), compiled in with `include_bytes!` and put in place of Bevy's default font, so every `TextFont` uses it without naming it. Set `weight: FontWeight::BOLD` for text read at a glance (`hud::bold`). It has a degree sign, so angles print as `Angle` displays them ("22.5°").
  - `meshes.rs`: every box is a `rounded_box`, whose soft edges give the toy look; its UVs are in meters. `lumber_mesh` draws a simulated board with crisp sawn edges, whatever its outline (mitered ends, corners cut out, sides beveled), and every slot, whatever plane its blade turned in and whichever way it traveled; resaw slots can leave the wood at a point in two spans or more. It lays out each region as it's seen from above (`Region::footprint`), and a leaning side holds its top down or its bottom up, so bevels are slanted tops and bottoms. Slots square to the face and resaws have simple shapes of their own; any other slot (a tilted one, or one whose blade traveled up through the board, like a board on edge crosscut partway) is a `Band`, the height the blade took between the slot's faces at each point of the face, solved from its disc, and cells split wherever those meet a face or each other; wooden furniture uses the rounded `board_mesh`. Use either with `ShopAssets::wood` (or `maple`) so the grain runs along the board. `Placed::grain` keeps the grain continuous as a board is cut.
  - `hand.rs`: carrying wood. There's one way to move wood anywhere: click it to pick it up (`Hand`), then click where it goes to set it down (`set_down`). What's carried or set aside is a `Workpiece`: one board, or a glue-up (an `Assembly`, lying flat as it lay on the bench, its `z` up). Boards are picked up off either saw, the workbench (with Space, since clicks there pick surfaces; Space takes a whole glue-up), or the lumber rack (new stock, sized like the board clicked); they're set down on a saw, the workbench, or in the scrap bin, or put away in the inventory. A glue-up goes anywhere a board does. Clicking a station sets the board down there, from the overview too. A press that moves more than a few pixels is a drag, not a click (`Gesture`), so dragging a board along the table saw never picks it up.
  - `inventory.rs`: the wood that isn't on a station or in hand (`Inventory`), on two shelves (`Shelf`): parts (what a cut was measured to make, glue-ups, boards put away whole) and offcuts (the other side of a cut, strips a cut set loose); a piece moves to the other shelf with a click. New lumber comes in the rack's sizes (`LUMBER`), as much as you like. From the inventory, a piece goes straight to the station you're at: onto a saw (setting aside what was there) or onto the bench; from the overview, into your hand. The saws keep what they set aside in their own list (`take_set_aside`), which `collect_set_aside` puts away each frame: whatever a board set down displaced, the other side of a cut when you take one side, and strips a cut set loose. At a saw, Store (C) puts everything on it away, and Cut again (A) puts the measured piece away and sets the offcut back against the fence or stop, to rip strip after strip or cut piece after piece. X puts away what's in hand; C at the bench puts away the picked part or glue-up.
  - No wood disappears: anything a station sets aside goes into the inventory. The scrap bin, and the inventory's scrap button, are the only ways to throw wood away.
  - Put game logic that doesn't need a window into plain structs like the station states so `cargo test -p game` can cover it.

## Look and performance

The look is bright and toylike, like Two Point Museum, not realistic, and the game must run well on modest hardware. One shadowed sun, a cool ambient fill, plain painted materials, MSAA, and tonemapping; no post-processing beyond that. Nothing is metallic: there's no environment map, so metal would render black.

Run `WOODSHOP_BENCH` before and after anything that adds rendering cost (new lights with shadows, post-processing, transparency, big meshes). For reference, on an M5 Pro with nothing else running the overview takes about 3.5 ms a frame at 4K (about 5.5 ms with the desktop sharing the GPU); the earlier realistic look (volumetric light, depth of field, SSAO, TAA) took 34 ms. Compare numbers measured under the same conditions.

## Commands

- `cargo test -p woodsim`: fast, run constantly. No Bevy compile needed.
- `cargo run -p game`: run the game.
- `cargo run -p game --features dev`: same, with Bevy dynamic linking for faster rebuilds.
- `WOODSHOP_SCREENSHOT=screenshots/shop.png cargo run -p game`: render a few frames offscreen, save a screenshot, quit. Use this to check visual changes yourself, then look at the image. It opens no window, so it works while the screen is locked.
  - To capture a station up close, temporarily `insert_resource(ShopView::TableSaw)` (or `MiterSaw`) before the plugins are added.
  - To check an interaction, temporarily add a `PreUpdate` system ordered `.after(bevy::picking::PickingSystems::Last)` that calls `Actions::start` (or `hold`) on some frame. Earlier than that, `read_keys` clears it before the game sees it. Headless runs have no pointer, so a tooltip only shows if you temporarily force its `display`. To capture a later moment, temporarily change the frame numbers in `screenshot_and_exit`. Remove all of it afterward.
- `WOODSHOP_BENCH=1 cargo run -p game --release`: render the overview offscreen at 4K as fast as possible, print frame times, quit. Offscreen, frames aren't held to the display's refresh rate, so this measures what a frame costs.
- `cargo clippy --workspace --all-targets` and `cargo fmt --all` before finishing.

## Writing Bevy code

Bevy breaks its API every release, and much of what you remember is from older versions. Before using any Bevy API you aren't certain about, check the 0.20 source:

- Examples: `~/.cargo/registry/src/*/bevy-0.20.0/examples/` (e.g. `3d/3d_scene.rs`, `window/screenshot.rs`)
- Sub-crate source: `~/.cargo/registry/src/*/bevy_<name>-0.20.0/src/`

Examples of things that differ from older Bevy:

- Events are now "messages" (`MessageWriter`, `commands.write_message`).
- Picking events are their own types (`PointerDrag`, `PointerDragStart`, `PointerOver`), not `Pointer<Drag>`. Observers take `On<E>`, and `MeshPickingPlugin` isn't part of `DefaultPlugins`.
- Text size is `TextFont { font_size: FontSize::Px(18.0), .. }`.
- 0.20 has a new scene macro (`bsn!` / `bsn_list!`). This project uses plain `commands.spawn((...))`; don't mix in BSN without asking.
- A spawned tuple can hold at most 15 components; add the rest with `.insert((...))`.
- Shadows respect `RenderLayers` but lighting doesn't: a light shades every mesh, whatever its layer.
- `Screenshot::image` captures of an image render target came out black here; `main.rs` reads the image back with `ReadbackOnce::texture` instead. A camera that renders to an image also needs `IsDefaultUiCamera`, or the UI isn't drawn.
- Runtime-made images have no mipmaps unless you build them (`textures::with_mipmaps`); without them, textures shimmer at a distance.

## Simulation rules

- `Length` is an exact integer count of nanometers (1" = 25,400,000 nm), chosen so 1/64", 0.001", and 0.01 mm are all exact. Never store a length the simulation depends on as a float. Convert with `as_meters_f32()` only when handing values to Bevy (1 Bevy unit = 1 meter).
- A board's length runs along the grain. Ripping (table saw) cuts the width; crosscutting (miter saw) cuts the length.
- Positions on a board are in its own coordinates: `l` along its length, `w` across its width, `t` up through its thickness. A board lies on a saw as its `Orientation` says, kept as the turn from its axes onto the saw's (`rotation`): any of the 24 square ways, each comparing equal to itself however it was reached, or any way at all (`Orientation::from_rotation`, `is_square`). `Assembly::resting` lays a board (as a glue-up of one) or a glue-up on a surface you pick, flat on the table, turned so another faces the fence as squarely as it can (a bevel touches it along its edge), and refuses (`Unsteady`) a surface for the fence that faces straight up or down, or where wood would reach past the one it rests on (it would rock) or past the one to the fence (it would meet the fence first); resting on each square way's down and fence surfaces gives back that way exactly (`tests/resting.rs`). Turned out of square, `across`, `along`, and `up` give the board's axis most nearly that way. Tipping (`tipped`) rolls it a quarter turn about its length, standing (`stood`) turns it a quarter turn about its thickness, and flipping and turning are about the saw's along and up; `across`, `up`, and `along` say which of its axes (`Axis::Length`, `Width`, `Thickness`) lies each way, and `on_edge` and `on_end` which it stands on. Tipped onto an edge, it stands on its `w = 0` edge with its `t = 0` face toward the fence, and stood up from there, on its `l = 0` end with that face still to the fence, its width along the table; to resaw from the other edge with the same face to the fence, flip it and turn it end for end. Unrolled and unturned, the table saw holds the `w = width` edge against the fence and leads with `l = 0`, and the miter saw puts the `l = length` end against the stop and the `w = width` edge against the fence. Turned a quarter turn and pushed by the miter gauge, the table saw crosscuts: the board's near side rests against the gauge's stop, which measures the piece from the blade's other side.
- The miter gauge swings 60° either way (`TableSaw::gauge_angle`, `MAX_GAUGE`; positive swings the blade's front, where the board meets it first, toward the stop). The board stays square against the gauge's face, so the blade crosses it at the angle, and the piece against the stop comes out the set length to its long point, as at the miter saw. `TableSaw::reach` is how far the board reaches along the blade's path as it lies: a pass that's to go all the way through feeds that far plus the blade's radius. `tests/gauge.rs` checks the set length and conservation along the gauge's edge at any angle, and cuts a mitered piece again every way it lies.
- A setting misses the board (`CutError::MissesBoard`) only where the blade would pass wholly beyond its corners (`reaches_blade`, exact), not wherever it's past the box around the board: slanted, a blade set to a mitered board's full length still crosses it, as when a frame side's second miter is cut at the same stop with the first's long point against it. The error gives the setting that would just reach the board. A pass that only grazes a corner turns a sliver narrower than the kerf to sawdust and leaves the board `Kerfed`, its outline as it was with the slot in it.
- Each table saw guide warns when it can't hold the board straight, as a real shop would: the rip fence when the board rides it on its short side (wider across than it is long along the fence), and the miter gauge when the board is longer along the cut than it is wide. The game builds a board's mesh in its own axes (`w`, `t`, `l` along x, y, z) and each station turns it into place.
- A board is its face, through its whole thickness, plus the `Kerf`s cut into it: slots a round blade swept without cutting it apart. The face is convex `Region`s bounded by `Edge`s (`face.rs`): one rectangle until a cut leaves some other shape, like a mitered end or a corner cut out. `length` and `width` are the rectangle around it, so a mitered board's length is to its long points.
- A side can lean, as a tilted blade would leave it (`Edge::lean`): the wood is where `out · (l, w) + lean * t <= at`, so the board is narrower at one face than the other. A side square to the faces has `lean` zero and is exactly the edge it always was, so boards with no leaning sides keep their regions and their fast 2D arithmetic. A region with a leaning side is a convex solid between the board's bottom face and its top (`Region::solid(thickness)` gives a `Solid` that works either way): its edges are just the sides it has a face on, sorted, and its corners are wherever three of its planes meet, each an exact fraction, with which side of a plane a corner is on worked out exactly in 256-bit sums (`wide.rs`). Clipping, subtracting a hole, whether two regions hold together (a face shared with some area), merging, bounds, and containment all go through `Solid`; one that loses its last leaning face is a plain region again, and a piece's thickness is what its wood spans, so a ridge between two bevels is thinner than the board. `Board::corners`, `Board::volume`, `Region::corners_through` (its corners in 3D) and `Region::footprint` (its outline from above, which the game draws from) work for both, and `Board::bevels` lists the sides leaning 45° or less with their angles, and `Board::tapers` the rest (`Edge::tapers`): sides leaning more than 45° are faces that slope, as a tilted resaw of a board on edge leaves, with how far they slope from the faces; `Direction::tilted` gives a tilted face's `out` and `lean`, within about 1/16,000,000 of a radian.
- A slot is what one round blade swept, in whatever plane it turned (`Kerf`): its faces are where `normal · (l, w, t)` is `near` and `far`, its center traveled `along` (in that plane) from `start` to `end`, and its center's path is where `up · (l, w, t)` is `center`, `up` being `normal × along`. `normal` and `along` are `Vector`s, three whole numbers with no common factor, and positions along them are in nanometers times their length, so they stay whole numbers as pieces move (`Kerf::moved_by`). A blade standing square to the face (`across()` is `Across::Face`, `normal.t` zero) runs along the board (a stopped rip, a groove, a rabbet), across it (a dado), or slanted (a scar from a miter saw turned to an angle), and came through the bottom face or the top; one parallel to the faces, through a board on edge, cuts a resaw slot (`Across::Thickness`), in through an edge; a tilted blade, anything between. Those square to the face and resaws keep the exact arithmetic they always had; any other slot is worked out from its plane the same way for every orientation. `Board::slot` measures any slot as you would, from the side the blade came in through (`Kerf::into`: a face, an edge, or an end, whichever the blade's up runs most nearly along): how wide, how deep into the wood, and how far it runs on that side and the one opposite. `Kerf::meets` is where a slot meets any plane through the board, and `Board::opening` is that cut short where the wood ends: statuses and plans describe and draw slots from these, so a board on edge crosscut partway is "a 1/8"-wide, 11/16"-deep kerf in an edge, 11/32" long".
- The table saw's blade tilts up to 45° (`TableSaw::tilt`, `MAX_TILT`), leaning away from the fence going up, as most cabinet saws do. It tilts about where it meets the table, so the fence and the gauge's stop still measure to there: ripped against the fence, the piece is the setting wide on its bottom face and wider on top; against the gauge's stop, it's the setting long on the bottom and shorter on top. `height` is how high the teeth reach either way. Tilted with the gauge turned too, it cuts a compound miter, mitered at the gauge's angle in the face. A tilted blade's face can still cross a board whose box it's past (the stop set past the end, the face leaning back in by the top), so whether it reaches is worked out from the board's corners. Tilted, it cuts whatever a square blade does: a groove short of the top leans too, a board on edge is resawn on a slant, and glue-ups come apart beveled. `tests/tilt.rs` checks the widths and lengths at the table, volumes, and with proptest that every piece replays and that, on a grid through the board, exactly one piece has each bit of wood the slot didn't take; `tests/history.rs` mixes tilted rips into its random cuts, and `tests/tilt.rs` glues two 45° bevels into a square corner. Plans say "blade 1" high and tilted 45°", cut lists and statuses "beveled 45° along one edge" (`describe_bevels`) or "tapered 3° across its width" (`describe_tapers`), and the bench and plans call a sloping face a "tapered face".
- What a slot holds is exact (`Kerf::contains`, in the blade's own plane, in 256-bit sums), and where it goes through a region is the slab between its faces as far along as the blade reached all the wood between them up its plane, a nanometer short (`Kerf::hole`, the planes around it). Whether a slot touches wood where either leans is worked out in floating point, to well within a nanometer. The tests in `solid.rs`, `kerf.rs`, and `board.rs` make tilted slots directly, and check with proptest that a tilted cut anywhere loses just the wood in its slot (pieces' volumes plus the slot's add up to the board's) and that, on a grid through the board, exactly one piece has each bit of wood the slot didn't take.
- Every board woodsim hands back is one piece. After any cut, `Board::pieces` takes wherever a slot goes all the way through out of each region of the board's face and groups what's left by which regions share some of a face: a slot the board's length splits it, a miter splits it on the slant, and a rip and a crosscut that meet cut out the corner between them. Earlier kerfs move into the pieces they touch, and each `Piece` says where its corner was on the board that was cut, through the thickness too. A cut through an earlier slot can set strips loose (`Cut::loose`).
- `Board::pieces` also splits a board where slots facing the same way, sharing some of their width, together swept all the wood between them (`layers`, `swept_through`): resaws from one pass with the blade higher than the board is wide, or from opposite edges that meet, split it into layers, and grooves from both faces whose depths together go through split it too. Where they swept is outlined just inside the blades' round ends, so a partial pass never splits off a sliver the blade didn't quite reach. A slot that goes through what's left then divides that. A split that leaves the board in one piece as big as ever doesn't count: the slots are just slots.
- Each saw works out its blade's sweep once, in its own frame (`TableSaw::sweep`, `MiterSaw::sweep`: a `Sweep` is the plane the blade turns in, whatever its angle and tilt, the face the setting measures to, and the path its center takes), and `Laid` carries it into every board on the saw, however that board lies: a board on its own is cut as a glue-up of one, so there's no code for one way a board lies and not another. A board on edge can be crosscut at the table saw (its thickness along the feed, the blade traveling through it) and chopped at an angle at the miter saw (the end leans across the thickness). A board or a glue-up can lie any of the 24 square ways, on end too: a board on end against the fence takes a tenon's cheek, and a block shorter than the blade is high is resawn through its length (`tests/on_end.rs`).
- The miter saw's table turns 50° either way (`MiterSaw::miter`; positive swings the blade's front toward the stop) about where the blade's stop-side face crosses the fence, so the piece against the stop comes out exactly the set length along the fence. The stop touches a board where it reaches furthest toward it: a mitered end's long point. `Board::miters` reads a board's slanted ends back.
- How wide a board the miter saw cuts through is how far its blade reaches at the bottom of its stroke (`MiterSaw::reach`): 7-1/2" out from the fence for the 12" saw, square, and less across the fence at an angle (`MiterSaw::widest`). A wider board, like one turned across the saw or a 6" board at 45°, it cuts as far as it reaches and leaves in one piece, and the station warns before the stroke.
- The miter saw's blade swings down on an arm from a pivot behind the fence (`MiterSaw::pivot`, `arm`, `stroke`), so its center moves on an arc. `MiterSaw::chop` takes how high the blade's lowest point stopped: below the table it cuts through (`crosscut` is a full stroke), and above it, by hand or at the depth stop, it scars the board from above. The saw doesn't slide, so a scar's bottom is curved like the blade and it reaches across only part of a wide board. A stroke leaves one kerf: the blade at the deepest point it reached, which covers what the arc swept on the way down. The station recuts from the board as it was each time the blade goes deeper, even after it breaks through: at the moment it first does, the blade's curve hasn't cleared the corners of the cut yet, and stopping there would leave a wedge of wood and a scrap of kerf on one piece's end.
- Tool settings and travel are exact, including the miter saw's depth stop and its angle (`Angle` counts hundredths of a degree). Lengths that come from the round blade (where a stopped slot ends) are exact to the nanometer and shown rounded to 1/32", as you'd measure them.
- A slanted direction is a pair of whole numbers within 1/16,000,000 of a radian of its angle, and every edge crosses the face at a whole number, so which side of a cut a point is on, and whether two pieces still hold together, are exact. Lengths along a slant land within a few nanometers of the ideal angle's. A blade's path has to be exactly square to its faces in whole numbers (`Kerf::blade`), and small enough that positions along it stay in a `Length`: for a board turned out of square, `Vector::nearest_pair` finds such a pair nearest the true directions, each within 1/16,000,000 of a radian, by lattice reduction (the whole-number directions square to the faces' normal, made short, then the nearest by continued fraction). They leave gaps around the short ones, so where the path falls in one, the normal is rounded a little differently; a part that's exactly zero stays zero, so a slot square to a board's face stays square to it. Keep the exact predicates in `face.rs` on `i128`; measurements for people can use `f64`.
- Every cut loses up to one kerf to sawdust. `Cut::piece` is the measured side (against the fence, or the length cut to).
- An `Assembly` holds `Member`s, each a board turned by a `Rotation` (whole numbers over 2^40, so quarter turns stay exact and frames, boxes, and cabinets stay square) and set with its corner at a whole-nanometer point. Two assemblies go together at a `Surface` of each (a board's bottom, its top, or a side through its thickness, named by its outline edge: an end, an edge, a miter) as a `Mating` says: which of the four ways around the joint (`turn`, each a quarter turn further about it) and how far across it (`slide`, along the two directions `Assembly::extent` gives, from where the surfaces' least sides line up). `Assembly::stops` gives the slides that are flush with one side, centered, and flush with the other. `Assembly::mating` works out the `Motion` that puts the other assembly there, and `Assembly::attach` adds it, renumbering its members after this one's. Attaching fastens every other place the two now meet flush too, as a glue-up gets glue on every face that meets: the last piece of a frame is glued at both corners. A place that's open, even a little, isn't fastened, and the bench says so. The surfaces face each other exactly at any angle, so two 50° miters meet at 80° and an octagon's at 135°. `Assembly::fit` measures every pair of surfaces that face each other within 1/8": how far apart across where they overlap, from least to most, negative where one runs into the other. A joint that's put together closes, the way a clamped one would, so cuts that don't match show up further on: a miter off by a degree leaves the corner a degree out of square, and a frame whose sides don't match won't close at its last corner. `tests/assembly.rs` builds frames from real miter saw cuts, including an octagon.
- A saw cuts a glue-up (`TableSaw::feed_glue_up` and `rip_glue_up`, `MiterSaw::chop_glue_up` and `crosscut_glue_up`), lying as a `Rotation` lays it on the saw, any way at all (`Rotation::about` turns by any angle about any axis): its axes onto along the table, across it toward the fence, and up, the way `Orientation::rotation` lays a board. The saw works out its blade's path in its own frame, as it does for a board; each board gets the slot that path cuts through it, in its own coordinates and at its own angle (exact for a board lying square on the saw, within 1/16,000,000 of a radian for one turned any other way), and comes apart the way boards do. Pieces of boards that were fastened together stay fastened wherever they still meet flush, and the result is sorted like a board's: the biggest part on the measured side, the biggest on the far side, and anything else loose (`GlueUpPass`, `GlueUpCut`). Its boards can be turned any way in it, like a board glued onto another's bevel. `tests/glue_ups.rs` checks that a glue-up of one board cuts exactly like the board, every flat way it lies on both saws, and that a 1x2 glued onto a 1x6's 30° bevel crosscuts with it into two glue-ups still glued, and rips wherever the blade reaches either board.
- A `Recipe` (`recipe.rs`) is wood written out exactly, one thing to a line in words and whole numbers: each board as its history (`stock`, then each `pass` with the saw's every setting, how the wood lay, and how far it went, and each `sawn` piece with its exact slot), named once and shared as it's shared, then the `board` or a glue-up's `member`s and `joint`s, and optionally a `saw` line for how a saw's set up for it now. Reading it cuts each board again from its stock, keeping its history; `tests/recipe.rs` checks writing what's read gives the same text.
- Every board knows where it came from (`Board::origin`, `history.rs`), so a project can be made again in a real shop the way it was made here: new lumber (`Source::Stock`), or a piece of another board a saw cut (`Source::Sawn`: the `Operation`, which is the saw with every setting, how the wood lay, and how far it went; the kerf; which part of what it left; and where that part sat on the board, on which `Side` of the cut: the measured piece against the fence or stop, the offcut, or a loose strip). The pieces of one cut share its operation, and one pass through a glue-up is one operation for every board it cut. Operations and joints (`Joint::number`) take numbers from one counter in the order they happen; the joints fastened at once share one, and a glue-up that's cut keeps its joints' numbers. Where a board came from doesn't change what it is: equality and hashing ignore it, as `Joint` equality ignores its number, so identical boards still count together. `Origin::replay` makes a board again by cutting its stock the same way, `at_on_stock` says where it sat in its stock, and `tests/history.rs` checks with proptest that every board any cuts leave replays exactly and sits within its stock. A new way of cutting has to go through `Board::sawn`, so it's recorded.
- Wood is conserved: piece + offcut + waste always equals the original dimension, and a pass that doesn't cut through leaves the dimensions alone and adds exactly one kerf. `tests/conservation.rs` checks this with proptest, `tests/miters.rs` and `tests/gauge.rs` check it along the fence for cuts at any angle, and `tests/on_edge.rs` through the thickness for resaws, `tests/glue_ups.rs` across and along glued-up panels, with `Board::has_wood_in` sampling that no two pieces share wood; `tests/any_lie.rs` does the same for a board, an L glue-up, or a board glued onto another's bevel at any angle, lying each of the 24 square ways or turned any way at all on either saw, at any gauge angle, tilt, and miter, stopped partway or all the way through, and checks each piece replays and sits where it was cut. Any new operation needs the same kind of test. Saws return a `Pass` (missed, kerfed, or cut apart); tests that expect a cut use `CutApart::cut_apart` from `tests/common`.
